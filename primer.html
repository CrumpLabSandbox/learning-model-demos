Reading the equations

Learning models are written as equations. This page shows you how to read them, one piece at a time, with things you can move and try. You do not need any maths beyond adding, subtracting, and multiplying.

New to this? The overview slides show what this page covers. If decimals or negative numbers feel rusty, do the maths warm-up first. Any unfamiliar word is in the glossary.

1. What an equation is doing

An equation in a learning model is a rule. It says how to update what the learner knows after one trial. The computer applies the same rule on every trial, over and over. Each time, the numbers that come out of one trial go into the next.

So you never have to "solve" the equation to use the model. You only need to know what each piece stands for and what the rule does with it. The rest of this page builds that up using the Rescorla-Wagner rule as the example:

2. Who sets each number

Every symbol in a learning equation is one of three kinds. Knowing which kind you are looking at answers most questions about an equation.

  • experimenter Set by the experimenter's design. Which cues are on a trial, and whether the outcome happens.
  • parameter Set by the modeller. Numbers chosen before training that control how the model behaves, such as how fast it learns. They do not change during training.
  • computed Calculated by the model. Everything else. These are what the model predicts, and they change from trial to trial.

Every model page on this site colours symbols the same way: green for the experimenter, purple for parameters, and pink for computed values. Use the buttons above to light each kind up.

3. Letters are names for numbers

A letter in an equation is a name for a number, the way a column heading in a spreadsheet names the numbers under it. V stands for associative strength: how strongly a cue predicts the outcome. Strength can be positive (the cue predicts the outcome), zero (it predicts nothing), or negative (it predicts the outcome will not happen).

The small letter written below and to the right is a subscript. It says whose number this is. VA is the strength of cue A, and VB is the strength of cue B. They are separate numbers that can change independently.

4. The Greek letters

Greek letters are not special maths. They are just more names, used by convention so that parameters and special quantities stand out from the ordinary letters. Here are the ones you will meet, and how to say them.

Capital Δ and Σ are labels for operations. The lower-case letters α, β, λ, θ, and γ stand for numbers.

5. Δ means "change in"

ΔV is read "delta V" and means the change in V on one trial: the value after the trial minus the value before. If V went up, ΔV is positive. If it went down, ΔV is negative.

The learning rule calculates ΔV. Then the model applies it with a second, very simple step: the new strength is the old strength plus the change. This site writes that with an arrow, ←, read "becomes".

6. Σ means "add up"

ΣV is read "sigma V" and means add up the strengths. In Rescorla-Wagner it adds up only the cues that are present on this trial. That total is the model's prediction of the outcome. Tick and untick the cues to see which ones go into the sum.

7. Side by side means multiply

When symbols are written next to each other with nothing between them, they are multiplied. αβ means α × β. A bracket is worked out first, then multiplied like any other number. So αβ(λ − ΣV) means: work out λ − ΣV, then multiply it by α and by β.

8. The error: what happened minus what was predicted

The bracket (λ − ΣV) is the heart of Rescorla-Wagner. λ is what happened, and ΣV is what the cues predicted. Subtracting one from the other gives the prediction error: how surprising the trial was. Its sign tells you the direction of learning, and its size tells you how much.

9. Trial numbers

Sometimes an equation needs to say which trial a value belongs to. Papers usually write the trial number as a small n above the letter: Vn is the strength on trial n, and Vn+1 is the strength on the next trial. The update step then reads VAn+1 = VAn + ΔVAn: the strength on the next trial is the strength on this trial plus this trial's change.

Careful: in most algebra, a raised number is a power, so x2 means x × x. In learning models a raised n is usually a trial counter, not a power. Read the text around an equation to see which one is meant.

Run the rule one trial at a time and watch each row's answer become the next row's starting value.

10. Where the curve levels off

Because the error shrinks as V gets closer to λ, each step is smaller than the one before. The curve rises quickly at first, then flattens. The value it approaches but never quite reaches is called the asymptote. In Rescorla-Wagner the asymptote is λ.

11. Working out where learning stops

You can find where a model ends up without running a single trial. Learning stops when nothing changes, which means every ΔV is zero. So: set Δ to zero and solve. This is the most useful trick for seeing what an equation implies. It explains overshadowing and conditioned inhibition in a few lines.

Pick an example and reveal the steps one at a time. Try to guess each step before you show it.

13. Absolute value: size without sign

Sections 13 to 15 are for the attention models, Mackintosh and Pearce-Hall. Skip them until you get there.

Two straight bars around something, as in |λ − ΣV|, mean "how big is this, ignoring whether it is positive or negative". This is called the absolute value. |−0.4| is 0.4, and |0.4| is also 0.4.

The attention models use it because surprise has a size but, for attention, no direction. An outcome that is 0.4 bigger than expected and one that is 0.4 smaller are equally surprising.

14. When attention changes

In Rescorla-Wagner, α (how much attention a cue gets) is a parameter: the modeller picks it before training and it never changes. The attention models change exactly that. In Mackintosh's and Pearce and Hall's models, α is computed: the model updates it after every trial on which the cue appears.

The modeller still picks a starting value. It is written with a superscript 0, as in α0, read "alpha on trial 0". Remember from section 9 that a superscript here counts trials; it is not a power.

Here is the same experiment through all three models. A is trained alone, then B is added. The chart shows attention to B.

The two attention models disagree about why attention changes. In Mackintosh's model attention goes to the best predictor. In Pearce and Hall's model it goes to cues whose consequences are still uncertain. Both lower attention to B here, for different reasons. How fast attention moves is set by θα (theta-alpha) in Mackintosh's model and by γ (gamma) in Pearce and Hall's.

15. A bar over a letter

In the Pearce-Hall model each cue has two strengths. V is excitatory strength: how strongly the cue predicts that the outcome will happen. V̄, with a bar over it, is inhibitory strength: how strongly the cue predicts it will not happen. Read V̄ as "V-bar".

What the cue predicts overall is the difference, V − V̄. Both strengths only ever grow, so extinction in this model does not erase learning: it adds inhibition on top.

16. Moments and states

SOP runs in moments, small steps of time inside a trial. Each stimulus is a set of elements, and at each moment every element is in one of three states: inactive, primary activity (A1), or secondary activity (A2).

Two kinds of p appear, and the subscript says which is which:

  • A proportion: pA1,A is the share of A's elements in A1 right now, from 0 to 1. Read the subscript as "state A1, stimulus A". It changes every moment.
  • A chance per moment: p1 is the chance that an inactive element becomes active while the stimulus is on; pd1 and pd2 are the chances of decaying from A1 and from A2 ("d" for decay). These are parameters.

Elements go round one way: inactive → A1 → A2 → inactive. So the three proportions always add up to 1.

17. Adding up over moments

In SOP a cue learns from overlap: how much it and the US are active at the same moments. At each moment, multiply the two proportions. If either is 0, that moment adds nothing. Then add up over every moment of the trial:

ΣtpA1,A⁢pA1,US

The t under Σ means "over every moment t". It is the same Σ as in Rescorla-Wagner, adding up over moments instead of over cues. The gain on a trial is L+ times this total; the loss is L− times the same kind of total with the US in A2.

18. Events as lists of numbers

MINERVA-AL has no strength for each cue. Instead it stores whole events. To do that, it writes each event as a vector: a list of numbers, one per feature.

Each stimulus has its own block of features (20 in the paper). A stimulus that is present fills its block with 1s; one that is absent leaves its block at 0. Stimuli that happen together are added. So Pj is feature j of the probe, Ej is feature j of the event, and Mij is feature j of memory trace i: the second letter in the subscript counts features, the first counts traces.

19. Similarity and the echo

When cues appear, MINERVA-AL compares them, the probe, with every trace in memory. Similarity S is the cosine: multiply the two lists feature by feature, add up, and divide by both lengths. It is 1 when they match, 0 when they share nothing, and −1 when one is the opposite of the other.

Each trace then counts in proportion to S3. Cubing keeps the sign and shrinks loose matches: 0.5 becomes 0.125, while 1 stays 1. The traces, each weighted this way, add up to the echo, which is scaled so its biggest feature is 1. How much of the outcome is in the echo, from −1 to 1, is written O|P: "the outcome given the probe".
