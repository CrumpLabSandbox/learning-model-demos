Judging contingency: people as learners

The models on this site were built for animals. This unit asks what they make of people judging what goes with what, using a line of experiments where a whole block of trials flashes by in seconds.

Sources: Crump, Hannah, Allan & Hord (2007); Allan, Hannah, Crump & Siegel (2008); Hannah, Crump, Allan & Siegel (2009); Siegel, Allan, Hannah & Crump (2009). The unit has three parts: the streamed-trial page (do the task), four phenomenon presets on every model page, and a tutorial.

The question people answer

Does eating shrimp go with a rash? Does the fertiliser go with growth? Does this square go with that circle?

In the lab: watch a cue and an outcome come and go, many times. Then rate how strongly they are related, from −100 to +100.

The animal's version is conditioning. The human version is contingency judgement.

Four numbers

Everything the person saw is in four counts: together (a), cue alone (b), outcome alone (c), neither (d).

ΔP is the chance of the outcome with the cue minus the chance without it. Pairings are not the same as contingency: try the second preset.

Students often weight cell a (pairings) too heavily and ignore cell d. The second preset (6, 24, 6, 24) has six pairings and ΔP = 0, which makes the point. The ΔP rule is from Allan (1980).

Streamed trials

Old procedure: one trial every few seconds, so a block took minutes and a person gave only a few ratings.

Streamed trials: 60 frames in about 12 seconds, each frame a square or not and a circle or not, then one rating. Dozens of ratings per person.

The same two findings came out: ratings follow ΔP, and the outcome density effect.

Try a stream yourself

Crump et al. (2007) used 100 ms frames with 100 ms black gaps. The site's page defaults to a slower pace and grey gaps, with the paper's settings available in the options.

What people do

  • Ratings follow ΔP. A stream with ΔP = 0.47 is rated above a stream with ΔP = 0.
  • Outcome density. Two streams with ΔP = 0 are rated −30 when the outcome is rare and +6 when it is common.
  • Cue interaction. A target with ΔP = 0.5 is rated −16 beside a companion that predicts perfectly and +25 beside one that predicts nothing.
  • Two-phase blocking, forward and backward, about equal in size, with probabilistic outcomes.

Rescorla-Wagner learns ΔP

Treat the stream itself as a cue, the context. On frames without the square, the context learns the outcome's base rate. On frames with it, the two share the error.

What is left for the square is the difference: ΔP. This is why the model was taken up for human judgements.

Chapman & Robbins (1990) derived the asymptote. The algebra is the fixed-point method from section 11 of the equations guide: set both changes to zero and solve. Without the context, the model learns P(O | cue) instead, which the tutorial shows.

A rating is a decision

Ask for "strong" or "weak" instead of a rating, and signal detection theory can separate sensitivity from the criterion.

Outcome density and the companion cue moved the criterion. Sensitivity did not change.

The models describe what is learned. The data say some famous effects live in how it is reported.

Allan et al. (2008) for outcome density, sign, and payoffs; Siegel et al. (2009) for one-phase blocking. The point for students: a model of learning and a model of deciding are different things, and the data need both.
