Maths warm-up: what to expect

For anyone whose maths feels rusty, or who finds maths stressful. You are in the right place.

Normalise the anxiety up front. Many psychology students have not done maths for years, and the models need only a few basic operations.

You need less maths than you think

Every model on this site uses only:

  • adding,
  • taking away,
  • multiplying by a number between 0 and 1,
  • and reading a graph.

No algebra to solve. No calculus. The computer does the arithmetic; you learn what it means.

The primer's section on "setting Δ to zero" is the only algebra on the site, and it is optional.

Why any maths at all?

A learning model is a rule that says how much to learn after each experience. Writing the rule with numbers makes it exact, so a computer can follow it and we can check whether it is right.

You do not need to do the sums yourself. You need to know what each number means, so you can tell why the model predicts what it does.

Emphasise meaning over calculation. The model pages always show the arithmetic already done.

What you will practise

  1. Numbers between 0 and 1, like 0.5 for a half.
  2. Numbers below zero, like winter temperatures.
  3. Finding the gap between two numbers.
  4. Taking part of a number, which is what multiplying by 0.5 does.
  5. Adding up, including negative numbers.
  6. Reading a graph: along, up, across.
  7. A rule, repeated: the idea every model here is built on.
Students comfortable with 1 to 5 can jump to 6 or 7.

The one idea to take away

Fill a glass where each pour fills part of the empty space. Try it:

Find the gap, take part of it, add it on. Repeat. That is how the first model learns.

This is a live widget. Pour a few times and point out that each pour adds less than the last, and the glass never overflows. That is the learning curve.

How the page works

  • Sliders to move. Nothing breaks; move them back any time.
  • Check yourself questions with a hint button.
  • Every answer explains itself, right or wrong.
  • Nothing is graded or saved.
If you use the warm-up in class, consider pairing students and having them explain each answer to each other in words.

If maths makes you anxious

  • Go slowly. One section at a time is fine.
  • Say each sum out loud in words: "one take away point three".
  • Use a calculator. Understanding beats speed.
  • A wrong answer is information, not a verdict.
  • Ask. Most people in the room are wondering the same thing.
Point students to campus support for maths if your institution has it.
