How do we learn what predicts what?

An introduction for people new to learning theory. No maths needed. Use the arrow keys or the buttons below to move through the slides.

This deck sets the scene before any equations. Aim: students leave knowing the vocabulary (cue, outcome, trial), two or three classic findings, and why anyone would turn an idea about learning into a model.

You do this all day

  • Your phone buzzes, and you expect a message.
  • You smell coffee, and you expect the café is open.
  • Dark clouds roll in, and you expect rain.
  • A song comes on, and you remember a summer.

One thing happens first and tells you something about what comes next. Learning these links is called associative learning.

Ask the class for their own examples before showing the list. Most people can list ten in a minute.

Pavlov's discovery

Around 1900, Ivan Pavlov was studying digestion in dogs. He noticed the dogs began to salivate before the food arrived, at signals that usually came before food.

He then paired a neutral sound with food many times. Soon the sound alone made the dogs salivate. They had learned that the sound predicts food.

Pavlov's main English-language account is his 1927 book, Conditioned Reflexes. The popular story says he used a bell; his lab used several signals, including a metronome. The point is the same either way.

The words researchers use

cueThe signal that comes first, such as the sound. Also called the conditioned stimulus, or CS. On this site cues are letters: A, B, C.
outcomeThe thing that matters, such as the food. Also called the unconditioned stimulus, or US.
trialOne go: the cue appears, and the outcome happens or it does not. A+ means cue A with the outcome; A− means A without it.
strengthHow strongly a cue predicts the outcome. Models give it a number.

Every one of these is in the glossary.

These four words carry most of the course. CS and US are worth knowing because the readings use them.

Learning has a shape

Acquisition. Pair a cue with the outcome and the response grows: quickly at first, then more slowly, until it levels off.

Extinction. Then present the cue without the outcome, and the response fades.

This chart is the real model's output, not a drawing. The negatively accelerated shape (fast, then slow) is one of the most reliable facts in learning.

A puzzle: pairing is not enough

In the late 1960s Leon Kamin trained rats that a noise predicted a mild shock. Then he played the noise and a light together, followed by the same shock.

The light was paired with the shock many times, yet the rats learned almost nothing about it. The noise already predicted the shock. This is blocking.

So learning is not just about things happening together. It is about surprise.

Kamin's experiments used conditioned suppression in rats. Blocking is the finding that motivated the Rescorla-Wagner model, the first model on this site.

Why build a model?

Words are vague

"Learning depends on surprise" sounds right, but how much surprise, and how much learning?

A model is exact

A model turns the idea into a rule precise enough for a computer to follow, trial by trial.

Exact means testable

The model predicts what should happen in any experiment, including new ones. Then we can check.

Being wrong is useful

When a model fails, we learn which part of the idea was wrong. That is how the next model is built.

The theme of the course: models make predictions explicit. A model that fails in a clear way teaches us more than a vague idea that can never fail.
