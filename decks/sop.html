SOP: what is active, moment by moment

Allan Wagner, 1981. The same Wagner as in Rescorla-Wagner, nine years later, asking a new question.

Wagner, A. R. (1981). SOP: A model of automatic memory processing in animal behavior. In Spear & Miller (Eds.), Information processing in animals: Memory mechanisms. The name stands for "sometimes opponent process". Computational details follow Mazur & Wagner (1982).

A new question

Rescorla-Wagner asks: how surprising was this trial?

SOP asks: what is active in memory at this moment?

A trial is no longer one step. It is a stretch of time, and what matters is what overlaps with what, moment by moment.

So for the first time in this course, the timing inside a trial and the gap between trials can change what is learned.

An everyday version

The doorbell rings. For a moment it is vivid: you are hearing it. That is primary activity, A1.

A few seconds later you are no longer hearing it, but it still echoes in your mind. That is secondary activity, A2.

While it is still echoing, a second ring feels less fresh. Something that is already in A2 cannot become vivid again straight away.

Three states

Each stimulus is a set of many small elements. At any moment each element is in one of three states, and they go round one way:

A1vivid, brief

A2fading, lingers

The model tracks the proportion of elements in each state, from 0 to 1.

Learning from overlap

Gain when the cue and the US are both in A1 at the same moments.

Loss when the cue is in A1 while the US is in A2, fading or only remembered.

Add up the overlaps over every moment of the trial. That is how much the link between the cue and the US changes.

There is no error term, no "outcome minus prediction". The overlap does the work.

Why learning levels off

A trained cue calls up the US. But a called-up US goes to A2, not A1.

Those elements are not free when the real US arrives, so the US is felt less: less gain, more loss.

Learning stops where gain and loss balance.

Timing matters

If the US comes long after the cue has gone, the cue's elements have already faded to A2. There is almost no overlap in A1, so almost nothing is learned.

Trials packed close together also learn less: the last US is still lingering in A2.

US first, cue second

When the cue comes after the US, the cue is active mostly while the US is fading in A2.

The loss beats the gain by a little on every trial, so over many trials the cue becomes an inhibitor. Rescorla-Wagner cannot tell the order apart.

Why "sometimes opponent"

The response is built from the US's activity: some from A1, some from A2. The two parts can push in the same direction or in opposite directions.

When they are opposite, a cue that calls up the US into A2 produces a response opposite to the response to the US itself. Cues that predict a drug, for example, can produce effects opposite to the drug's own.

On the model page the response is R = w1 × (US in A1) + w2 × (US in A2). Set w2 to a negative number in Everything view and tick "Show the response R" to see the opponent response. The drug example is Siegel's work on conditioned compensatory responses and drug tolerance.

What it gets wrong

  • Backward blocking. An absent cue's elements are inactive, so it learns nothing.
  • Negative patterning. A compound still calls up the sum of what its parts call up.
  • Many parameters. Different settings can change which effects appear, so its predictions need careful checking.

Later versions of SOP fix some of these. This site shows the 1981 model.
