Rescorla-Wagner: learning from surprise

Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner, 1972. The most influential model of associative learning, and the first on this site.

Rescorla, R. A., & Wagner, A. R. (1972). A theory of Pavlovian conditioning. In Black & Prokasy (Eds.), Classical Conditioning II.

The puzzle it solved

If A already predicts the outcome, adding B alongside it teaches almost nothing about B. This is blocking.

So simply pairing a cue with an outcome is not enough. Something else matters.

The big idea

You learn only when you are surprised.

On every trial, the learner predicts the outcome. If the outcome is bigger than predicted, the cues present gain strength. If it is smaller, they lose strength. If it is exactly as predicted, nothing changes.

The amount of surprise is called the prediction error. The same idea later became central in neuroscience, in work on dopamine signals.

An everyday version

Your phone buzzes and there is a message. The first few times, you learn "buzz means message".

Once you expect it, another buzz-then-message teaches you nothing new. There is no surprise left.

Now suppose your phone also lights up whenever it buzzes. You never learn much about the light, because the buzz already told you a message was coming. That is blocking.

Cues share one prediction

All the cues present add their strengths into one total prediction. They then share one surprise.

  • If A already predicts the outcome, the total is already right, so B gets nothing. That is blocking.
  • If A and B start together, they split the outcome between them. That is overshadowing.
On the model page, the "Summed error" switch turns this sharing off. Blocking and overshadowing disappear, which shows that this one assumption produces them.

Learning slows as it succeeds

Each trial closes part of the gap between what happened and what was predicted, like a pour filling part of the empty space in a glass.

Big gap, big step. Small gap, small step. So the curve rises fast, then levels off.

What it explains

Acquisition

Learning grows, fast then slow.

Extinction

Without the outcome, strength falls.

Blocking

A known cue stops a new one being learned.

Overshadowing

Cues trained together split the learning.

Conditioned inhibition

A cue can predict that the outcome will not happen.

What it gets wrong

  • Latent inhibition. Pre-exposure to a cue slows later learning, but in the model nothing changes when nothing is surprising.
  • Backward blocking. The model never changes a cue that is absent.
  • Negative patterning. The model's prediction for A and B together is always their sum.
  • Spontaneous recovery. Extinction erases learning in the model, so nothing can come back.

The later models on this site each try to fix one of these.

The failures are as instructive as the successes. On the model page these appear as cards marked "Does not", each explaining which part of the rule is responsible.

The rule, in words and symbols

The change in a cue's strength = how noticeable the cue is × how fast the outcome teaches × the surprise.

ΔVA = αA β ( λ − ΣV )
