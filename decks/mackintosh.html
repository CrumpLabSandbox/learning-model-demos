Mackintosh: attention to the best predictor

Nicholas Mackintosh, 1975. The first of two models on this site in which attention changes with experience.

Mackintosh, N. J. (1975). A theory of attention: Variations in the associability of stimuli with reinforcement. Psychological Review, 82, 276–298.

What Rescorla-Wagner left out

In Rescorla-Wagner, how noticeable a cue is (α) is fixed. Nothing that happens to a cue can change how easily it is learned about.

But experience does change it. A tone heard many times with nothing following it is later learned about slowly: latent inhibition.

What if attention itself is learned?

This is the motivation for both attention models. They keep the idea of learning from error but let α, renamed attention or associability, change.

The big idea

Pay attention to the cues that predict best. Tune out the rest.

After every trial, each cue present is compared with the other cues present. If it predicted the outcome better, attention to it rises. If it predicted worse, attention falls. A cue with more attention is learned about faster.

An everyday version

Your group chat and one friend both tell you when plans change. The friend is always right; the group chat is often late or wrong.

Soon you check your friend's messages first and barely glance at the group chat. You have stopped paying attention to the worse predictor, and you learn little from it even when it happens to be right.

Two rules

  1. Learning. Each cue learns from its own error: what happened minus what that cue alone predicted. Unlike Rescorla-Wagner, cues do not share one error.
  2. Attention. Attention to a cue rises if it predicted better than the other cues present, and falls if it predicted worse.

So cues compete only through attention.

Blocking, a different way

B still learns on the first compound trial, before its attention drops. Rescorla-Wagner says B should learn almost nothing even then.

Writing the rule down changes the predictions

Mackintosh said which way attention moves, but not by how much. The model page offers two ways to fill that in:

Blocking✓ shows it✗ does not
Overshadowing✗ does not✓ shows it

The difference is how a tie is treated. This is why models are written as equations: words alone hid this choice.

These results are for this site's settings, with equal saliences, and are checked by automated tests. Under the continuous rule a tie leaves attention unchanged; under the direction rule a tie lowers it. Overshadowing appears under the continuous rule too if one cue is more salient.

What it gets wrong

  • Conditioned inhibition. A cue's own error on trials without the outcome is zero, so it never goes below zero.
  • Backward blocking. Absent cues never change.
  • Negative patterning. A compound still predicts the sum of its parts.
