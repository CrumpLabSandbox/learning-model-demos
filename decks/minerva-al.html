MINERVA-AL: learning as remembering

Randall Jamieson, Matthew Crump, and Samuel Hannah, 2012. Built on Douglas Hintzman's memory model, MINERVA 2.

Jamieson, R. K., Crump, M. J. C., & Hannah, S. D. (2012). An instance theory of associative learning. Learning & Behavior, 40, 61–82. The site reproduces the paper's tables of simulation results in its automated tests.

No strengths at all

Every model so far keeps one number per cue, its strength, and updates it after each trial. The trials themselves are forgotten.

MINERVA-AL keeps every trial in memory, as its own trace. Nothing is ever updated.

Learning is remembering. Expecting is recalling.

An everyday version

You meet a dog in the park. Memories of other dogs come back, and the ones most like this dog come back most strongly.

What you expect, a wagging tail or a growl, is a blend of what happened with all of them, weighted by how alike they are.

Events as lists of numbers

Each stimulus has its own block of features: 1s when it is there, 0s when it is not. A trial is the stimuli on it, added together. Tick the boxes.

Recalling: the echo

The cues (the probe) are compared with every trace. Each trace counts in proportion to its similarity, cubed, and they add up to the echo: what the learner expects.

What gets stored: the surprise

Each trial does not store the event itself, but what happened minus what was expected.

  • An outcome that was expected leaves almost no mark.
  • An outcome that was not expected is stored strongly.
  • An outcome that was expected but did not come is stored as its opposite.

The paper calls this discrepancy encoding. Switch it off on the model page and blocking disappears.

Blocking

By the compound trials, A already brings back the outcome. The surprising thing on an AB trial is B, so each trace stores B strongly and the outcome only weakly.

Later, B brings back those traces, and with them little outcome.

A new cue starts well above zero here: its probe includes the context, which matches every trace a little, and the echo is scaled so its biggest feature is 1. The paper's Table 7 shows the same blocking effect.

Backward blocking

On the A+ trials, A brings back B from the old AB traces, but B is not there. So each new trace stores the opposite of B.

When B is tested, those traces match it negatively and take outcome away from its echo. No other model on this site can do this.

Negative patterning

AB is compared with every trace. The AB traces match it best, and cubing makes them count far more than the A and B traces.

So the compound is remembered as its own pattern, not as A plus B. The models built on summed strengths cannot do this.

Many learners

Which features are stored on each trial is random, so two simulated learners never learn quite alike.

The model page runs 25 simulated learners, as the paper did, and plots their average. The shaded band shows the spread. The memory view follows learner 1.

What it gets wrong

  • Time. Each trial is one event, so trial spacing, the gap between cue and outcome, and their order cannot matter. Compare SOP.
  • Unblocking with a bigger outcome is not produced in this site's design.
  • A brand-new cue tested in a familiar context already brings back some of the outcome.

Like every model here, it is a precise claim that can be tested, and its failures are informative.
