Delamater: a network that learns what the cues are

Andrew Delamater, 2012. A small neural network in which the representation of a cue is learned, not given.

Delamater, A. R. (2012). On the nature of CS and US representations in Pavlovian learning. Learning & Behavior, 40, 1–23. The article is a review of CS and US representation; the model is in its first half. The learning rule is backpropagation (Rumelhart, Hinton, & Williams, 1986) with momentum.

A question the other models skip

Every model so far takes the cues as given: a tone is a tone, and what is learned is a number attached to it.

But rats trained with two lights and two tones, each pair with its own food, come to treat the two lights as alike and the lights and tones as different. Acquired equivalence: experience changes what the cues are to the animal.

What if the representation itself is learned?

Three layers

Input units are features: one for each cue, one shared by the cues of a modality, one for the context.

Hidden units stand between. Each modality has a pathway of its own, and a multimodal pathway takes every input. They start out meaning nothing.

Outcome units, one per US. What they say is the prediction.

Figure 4 in the paper: two hidden units per modality pathway and four multimodal, with the stimuli coded as a distinct feature plus a shared modality feature (A1 = a + b, A2 = b + c). The site's Modalities line in a design does this coding.

Learning works backwards from the mistake

Each unit squashes its input through a smooth step, the logistic, shifted so that a quiet unit rests near 0.1.

At the outcome, the error is what happened minus what was said. That error is passed back along the connections: a hidden unit that fed a wrong answer gets most of the blame, and every weight moves to shrink the mistake. This is backpropagation.

What the hidden layer buys

Without a hidden layer the network is Rescorla-Wagner through a squash: it can only add up. With one, a compound can mean something its elements do not.

Negative patterning, biconditional discriminations, and occasion setting all become solvable, and the paper reproduces the order in which animals find them hard.

Switch off "Hidden layer" on the model page to see the one-layer network fail negative patterning. The paper's Figure 8 reproduces Harris et al. (2008): positive patterning is learned faster than negative patterning, and a biconditional discrimination slower than either.

More than one outcome

The outcome units are separate, so the network can learn that a tone means food and a light means sugar water. Write A+1 and B+2 in a design.

Cues reinforced with different outcomes come to use different hidden units; cues reinforced with the same outcome come to share them. That is the network's account of acquired equivalence and of why differential outcomes make hard discriminations easier.

The paper's Figure 5 (Delamater, 1998, Experiment 3): a reversal is learned faster when the two stimuli of a modality were trained with different USs. Figures 7 and 9: biconditional and ambiguous occasion-setting tasks are learned faster with differential outcomes. The tests reproduce these as orderings.

What it gets wrong, and what it costs

  • Latent inhibition. A trial with no outcome and no prediction produces no error, so nothing changes.
  • Timing. Like every trial-level model, it knows nothing about moments.
  • Biology. Delamater notes that neurons do not pass errors backwards. The rule is a stand-in for something more plausible that behaves alike.
  • Speed. The network needs hundreds of trials for the harder tasks, and eight runs are averaged because the starting weights are random.
