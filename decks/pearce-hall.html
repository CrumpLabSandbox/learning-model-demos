Pearce-Hall: attention to the uncertain

John Pearce and Geoffrey Hall, 1980. A second attention model, built on the opposite idea to Mackintosh's.

Pearce, J. M., & Hall, G. (1980). Psychological Review, 87, 532–552. The running average of attention used by default on this site is from Pearce, Kaye, & Hall (1982).

The opposite idea

Mackintosh: pay attention to cues that predict well.

Pearce and Hall: once a cue predicts well, there is nothing left to learn about it, so stop paying close attention. Keep attending to cues whose consequences are still uncertain.

Surprise drives attention. Attention drives learning.

An everyday version

With a new phone, every buzz grabs your attention. Once you know what each buzz means, you barely notice them.

Then one day a buzz means something unexpected, and you are back to noticing every one. Surprise brought your attention back.

Attention over time

Attention starts high: a new cue is uncertain. It fades as A comes to predict the outcome.

When extinction starts, the missing outcome is a surprise, and attention jumps back up.

Two kinds of strength

Each cue learns an excitatory strength V (the outcome will happen) and an inhibitory strength V̄ (it will not). What it predicts is the difference, V − V̄.

Both only ever grow. So extinction does not erase what was learned: it adds inhibition on top. That leaves room for old learning to come back.

Latent inhibition

During pre-exposure nothing follows A and nothing is expected, so there is no surprise and attention to A fades to zero.

When A is finally paired with the outcome, it starts with little attention and learns slowly. The new cue B starts with full attention.

Blocking: one trial, then attention collapses

B is new, so on the first compound trial it gets full attention and learns.

But A already predicts the outcome. No surprise, so attention to B collapses, and B learns little after that.

Late in training attention to D also fades, and ends a little below B's: C and D together come to predict the outcome almost exactly, while A and B together slightly over-predict it, so a trace of surprise remains on AB trials. What matters for blocking is the first few compound trials, when D still has attention and B does not.

Both explain blocking, by different routes. Researchers now think both kinds of attention may exist.

The last point is a hedge on purpose: hybrid models combining the two (for example Le Pelley, 2004) are an active topic. Results in the table are for this site's default settings and are checked by automated tests.

What it gets wrong

  • Backward blocking. Absent cues never change.
  • Negative patterning. A compound still predicts the sum of its parts.

The last model on this site, MINERVA-AL, handles both without any associative strength at all.
