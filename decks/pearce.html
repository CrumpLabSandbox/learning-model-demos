Pearce: the whole pattern learns

John Pearce, 1987. A configural model: what learns is not each cue but the whole pattern of stimulation on a trial.

Pearce, J. M. (1987). A model for stimulus generalization in Pavlovian conditioning. Psychological Review, 94, 61–73. The 1994 revision (Psychological Review, 101, 587–607) squares the similarity and adds a connectionist framing; this site implements the 1987 paper.

A problem the other models share

In every model so far, a compound predicts the sum of its parts. Tone predicts food, light predicts food, so tone-and-light must predict food even more.

But animals learn negative patterning: food after the tone, food after the light, no food after both. Adding up cannot do that.

What if the unit of learning is the whole pattern, not the parts?

The big idea

On a trial the learner meets one configuration: everything present together, in this place. That configuration is the thing that learns.

Tone alone, light alone, and tone-with-light are three different configurations, each with its own strength. So the compound can learn something the elements have not.

The configuration includes the context, which is why the model treats conditioning to a single cue as a discrimination between "cue plus context" and "context alone".

Similarity lends strength

If configurations were strangers to each other, a tone trained with a light would know nothing when tested alone. It does not; it knows less.

So each pattern borrows from every other in proportion to their similarity: the share of each pattern taken up by what they have in common.

With equal intensities and no context, a compound and either element have similarity 0.5, which is what Pearce's own simulations used. Intensity matters: the compound is more similar to its more intense element, so the fainter one is overshadowed more.

Overshadowing on the first trial

B tested alone is a different pattern from AB, so it borrows only part of AB's strength: generalisation decrement.

That is why overshadowing is there after a single trial, where Rescorla-Wagner needs many. Adding a new cue to a trained one dilutes it the same way: external inhibition.

Negative patterning

AB borrows excitation from A and from B, but it is not reinforced, so it learns inhibition of its own until the borrowed excitation is cancelled.

Rescorla-Wagner, Mackintosh, Pearce-Hall, and SOP all fail here. MINERVA-AL and Pearce solve it, in different ways.

Inhibition is new learning

When less happens than a configuration predicted, it does not lose excitation. It gains inhibition, which is subtracted from what it predicts.

So extinction leaves the old learning in place and lays new learning over it, and inhibition generalises to similar patterns just as excitation does.

The same view as Pearce-Hall, and Konorski before them.

Pearce's Figure 1 (A+ with AB−) and Figure 2 (A− with AB+) show the consequences: the reinforced element or compound ends up with more excitation than λ, "supernormal", balanced by inhibition borrowed from the nonreinforced pattern. The tests reproduce both figures' asymptotes exactly.

What it gets wrong

  • Latent inhibition. Nothing happens on a trial with no outcome and no prediction, so pre-exposure changes nothing. Pearce suggests attention would be needed.
  • Backward blocking. A configuration that is not presented does not change.
  • Timing. Like every trial-level model, it knows nothing about moments.
