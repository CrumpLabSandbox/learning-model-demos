Why blocking happens, five ways

Blocking is one of the most famous findings in learning. Every model on this site produces it, but each for a different reason. Seeing the five reasons side by side is a good way to see what each model is really claiming.

The finding

Kamin (1969) first trained rats with a noise, A, followed by a mild shock. Then he trained a compound of the noise and a light, AB, followed by the same shock. A comparison cue, D, was trained with a new partner, C. Here is the design, in this site's notation:

Pretraining: 20 A+ Compound: 20 AB+, 20 CD+

The rats learned little about B. A blocked it. Here is what the five models say, run just now:

1Rescorla-Wagner: a shared error

All the cues present share one prediction error, λ − ΣV. By the compound trials A already predicts the outcome, so ΣV is close to λ and the error is close to zero. B learns from that same tiny error. The term that blocks B is ΣV.

Try it: switch off "Summed error" and blocking disappears.

2Mackintosh: attention moves to the better predictor

Each cue learns from its own error, so B is not blocked by sharing A's error. Instead, A predicts the outcome better than B does, so attention to B falls, and with little attention B learns little. D's partner C is new too, so D keeps its attention. The term that blocks B is attention, α.

Try it: switch off "Attention changes".

3Pearce-Hall: no surprise, no attention

Pearce and Hall make the opposite claim about attention: we attend to cues whose consequences are still uncertain. On the first compound trial B is new and learns. But A already predicts the outcome, so there is no surprise, and attention to B collapses. Blocking comes from attention again, for the opposite reason.

4SOP: the gain and the loss cancel

In SOP a cue gains strength when it and the US are both in primary activity (A1), and loses strength when it is active while the US is in secondary activity (A2). A trained A calls the US up into A2 before it arrives, so little of the US reaches A1: B gains little and loses about as much. Blocking comes from timing in memory, with no error term at all.

5MINERVA-AL: store only the surprise

MINERVA-AL has no strengths. Each trial is stored as a trace: what happened minus what memory expected. On AB trials A already brings back the outcome, so each trace stores B strongly and the outcome only faintly. Later, B brings back those faint traces. Blocking comes from discrepancy encoding.
