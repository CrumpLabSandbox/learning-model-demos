Judging contingency: from ΔP to the learning rule

The models on this site were built to explain animals. Since the 1980s they have also been used to explain how people judge what goes with what. This tutorial follows four findings from one line of human experiments, the streamed-trial studies, and asks what each model makes of them. It ends with the part of a judgement that none of the models has: the decision.

The task, and the four numbers behind it

In a contingency judgement experiment a person watches a cue and an outcome come and go, then rates how strongly they are related, from −100 to +100. Everything the person could use is in four counts: how often the cue and the outcome came together (a), the cue alone (b), the outcome alone (c), and neither (d). This is the 2 × 2 table, and the standard measure of the relationship is ΔP: the chance of the outcome with the cue, minus the chance of the outcome without it.

Try the second preset. The cue and the outcome come together on 6 frames, but ΔP is 0: the outcome is just as likely without the cue. Pairings are not contingency. That distinction is the whole subject of this tutorial (the finding on its own terms).

In the streamed-trial procedure (Crump, Hannah, Allan & Hord, 2007) a whole block of 60 frames flashes by in about 12 seconds, then one rating is taken. Try it yourself before reading on: a stream or two is enough to feel what the task is like. Here is how a stream looks to this site's models. Each frame is a trial, and a context cue Z stands for the stream itself, so an outcome with no cue is + and a frame with nothing on it is -:

Positive: 17 A+, 13 A-, 3 +, 27 -, random Zero: 6 B+, 24 B-, 6 +, 24 -, random Context: Z

1Ratings follow ΔP, and so does Rescorla-Wagner

People's ratings rise with ΔP. In the first streamed-trial experiment, streams with ΔP = 0.47 were rated higher than streams with ΔP = 0. Here is the design above run through Rescorla-Wagner, with each stream's ΔP drawn as a dashed line:

Why ΔP? Because the context is a cue. On frames without A, Z is the only cue present, so Z learns how often the outcome comes without A. On frames with A, A and Z share one error, so between them they learn how often the outcome comes with A. What is left for A is the difference. If you set both changes to zero, as in section 11 of the equations guide, you get VZ = P(O | no A) and VA = ΔP. Chapman and Robbins (1990) first pointed this out, and it is the reason the model was taken up for human judgements.

2Outcome density: the same ΔP, rated differently

Take two streams with no contingency at all. In one the outcome is rare (it comes on 20% of frames), in the other common (80%). People rate the common one higher: about −30 for the rare stream and about +6 for the common one, though both have ΔP = 0. This is the outcome density effect, and it is one of the oldest findings in the area (the evidence).

At its asymptote Rescorla-Wagner predicts no outcome density effect: both cues reach ΔP. Averaged over several streams, so that the order of frames does not decide the answer, the models split:

Mackintosh, SOP, and MINERVA-AL show the effect, each because a common outcome means many pairings. Rescorla-Wagner and Pearce-Hall barely do. So is the effect in what people learn, or in how they answer?

Where the effect lives: a judgement is a decision

Allan, Hannah, Crump and Siegel (2008) asked people for a yes-or-no answer instead of a rating: was the relationship in this stream strong or weak? That makes the task a detection problem, and signal detection theory can separate two things. Sensitivity is how well a person tells strong streams from weak ones. The criterion is how much they need to feel before they say "strong".

Outcome density did not change sensitivity. People told a ΔP of 0.6 from a ΔP of 0.4 just as well whether the outcome was rare or common. What changed was the criterion: with a common outcome, people needed less to say "strong". The same was true when the sign of the contingency changed and when payoffs were imposed. The effect is in the decision.

This matters for reading the verdicts above. A learning model describes what the person takes in from the frames, the input. A rating is that, plus a decision about how to report it, the output. A model that shows the outcome density effect is putting in the input something the data place in the output. A model that does not show it is not thereby wrong: it may be describing the input correctly and leaving the decision to something else.

3Cue interaction inside one stream

Hannah, Crump, Allan and Siegel (2009) put two cues in a stream, a square and a triangle. The target had ΔP = 0.5 with the outcome in every stream. Its companion either predicted the outcome perfectly (ΔP = 1) or predicted nothing (ΔP = 0). People rated the target about −16 with a perfect companion and about +25 with a useless one. This is one-phase blocking: no pretraining phase, just two cues in one block (the evidence).

Companion predicts: 18 AB+, 6 A+, 6 B-, 18 -, random Companion useless: 9 CD+, 9 D+, 3 C+, 3 +, 3 CD-, 3 D-, 9 C-, 9 -, random Context: Z

For Rescorla-Wagner this is blocking by another name: A uses up the error on AB frames, so B learns little. What B settles at is its contingency given A, which is 0. Every model shows the effect, averaged over streams:

And yet Siegel, Allan, Hannah and Crump (2009) ran the signal detection version of this experiment too. People told the target's contingencies apart just as well with either companion. The companion moved the criterion. Once again the models place in learning an effect that the yes-or-no data place in the decision.

4Two phases, probabilistic outcomes, both orders

The classic blocking design trains the companion alone first. Shanks (1985) ran it with people and probabilistic outcomes, and Hannah and colleagues repeated it with streams. First the companion A alone is followed by the outcome on 3 frames in 4. Then B is added to A, and a comparison cue D is added to a new companion C. Each compound is followed by the outcome on 3 frames in 4 and each companion alone on 1 in 4, so B and D both have ΔP = 0.5 in the compound phase. (E is the control's own first-phase companion, which never appears again, as in the paper.) The finding has a page of its own.

People rated B lower than D, and more so the more often A had been followed by the outcome in the first phase. With probabilistic outcomes the models predict less than they do for the deterministic design:

People also show the effect in the backward order, with the single-cue phase second, and it is about the same size. Here the models fall away. A cue that is absent cannot change in Rescorla-Wagner, Mackintosh, Pearce-Hall, or SOP, so B is frozen the moment its last frame has passed. Two lessons. First, Rescorla-Wagner's success with human judgements is a success about ΔP: with a context cue, the rule is a running estimate of the contingency, and much of the human data follows ΔP. Second, a rating is not V. Between what a person learns and what they report sits a decision, and in the streamed-trial studies several famous effects turned out to live there. A model of learning and a model of deciding are different things, and the data need both.
